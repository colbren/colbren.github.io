Project 0: Becoming Friends with Your Camera

Part 1: Selfie: The Wrong Way vs. The Right Way

The close-up shot was taken from just a foot or two away, and it noticeably distorts the face: the nose and features nearest the lens look enlarged, while the ears and outline of the head appear to recede and shrink. This is perspective distortion at short focal distances, the parts of the face closer to the camera are a much larger fraction of the total distance to the lens than the parts farther away, so they subtend a disproportionately large angle and get magnified relative to the rest of the face. In the second shot, I stepped back several feet and zoomed in optically to make the face the same size in the frame. Because the subject is now much farther from the camera, the difference in distance between the nose and the ears is small relative to the total distance, so all parts of the face are imaged at roughly the same scale. The result looks much more natural and closer to how we perceive faces in everyday life.

Part 2: Architectural Perspective Compression

This is the same effect as Part 1, but now applied to a building instead of a face, and with the roles reversed. In the first photo, I stood far from the building and used the zoom to magnify it to roughly the size it appears in the second photo. Because the camera is so far away, the difference in distance between the near and far edges of the facade is tiny relative to the total distance, so the building's depth is effectively "compressed" receding surfaces and the sides of the building look flattened, almost like a cardboard cutout, and parallel lines stay nearly parallel. In the second photo, taken up close with no zoom, the near corner of the building is much closer to the camera than the far corner, so the difference in distance is now a large fraction of the total distance. This produces strong perspective: converging lines, an exaggerated sense of depth, and a much more three-dimensional look. The zoomed-out, far-away shot compresses perspective; getting physically close exaggerates it.

Part 3: The Dolly Zoom

The dolly zoom (a.k.a. the "Vertigo shot," named after its famous use in Hitchcock's Vertigo) combines the two effects above into a single, dynamic camera move: the camera physically moves toward or away from the subject (a "dolly") while simultaneously zooming in the opposite direction, so that the subject stays the same size in the frame throughout the shot. Since the subject's size is held constant but the camera's distance to the background is changing, the amount of perspective compression changes continuously — the background appears to stretch or warp dramatically around the subject, producing a disorienting, vertiginous feeling without the subject ever appearing to move.
