Project 4: Image Warping and Mosaicing

Part A

In this part I shoot overlapping photos by rotating the camera in place, recover the homography between each pair from point correspondences, and use inverse warping to rectify planar objects and stitch the photos into mosaics. Everything below is my own numpy code. The homography is a least-squares solve with np.linalg.lstsq, and both interpolation methods are written by hand. No findHomography, warpPerspective, skimage.transform or griddata is used. For bells & whistles, I project the mosaics onto a cylinder and a sphere, sampling directly from the original photos.

A.1: Shooting the Pictures

I shot three sets on my phone (2160×3840, portrait), rotating the camera between shots and keeping roughly 40–60% overlap. Between neighbors the camera turned 14–23°, which I measured in A.5. The scenes are a studio apartment, a building lobby with a large painted mural, and a street corner at night. The night set has four photos covering about 95° in total. Each photo spans about 49° horizontally.

The night photos vary a lot in exposure. The phone's night mode metered each shot differently, which comes up again in A.4. Set 1 also has objects about a meter from the camera (the hampers and the couch arm). A handheld phone never rotates exactly about its center of projection, so those objects show some parallax.

A.2: Recovering Homographies

A homography maps a point \((x, y)\) in one image to \((x', y')\) in the other:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} w x' \\ w y' \\ w \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x' = \frac{a x + b y + c}{g x + h y + 1}, \qquad y' = \frac{d x + e y + f}{g x + h y + 1} \]

Multiplying out the denominators makes both equations linear in the 8 unknowns, so each correspondence contributes two rows of \(A\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{b}\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x x' & -y x' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -x y' & -y y' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c & d & e & f & g & h \end{bmatrix}^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} \]

computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts) stacks these rows for all \(n\) points and solves the overdetermined \(2n \times 8\) system with least squares. With pixel coordinates in the thousands, the \(-x x'\) columns are around \(10^7\) while the constant columns are 1, which makes the system badly conditioned. So I first normalize each point set (Hartley normalization: subtract the mean, then scale so the mean distance from the origin is \(\sqrt{2}\)), solve, and undo the normalization: \(H = T_2^{-1} \tilde{H} T_1\).

Correspondences. Each pair has 20 correspondences spread across the overlap. To get precise sub-pixel locations, I did not click them by hand. Candidate matches were proposed by SIFT descriptor matching, my own RANSAC loop (built on computeH) kept the ones consistent with a single homography, and farthest-point sampling picked 20 of those that cover the overlap evenly. The points are saved as JSON, and my ginput-style tool (click_points.py) can view, edit or re-click any pair.

A.3: Warping the Images

warpImageNearestNeighbor(im, H) and warpImageBilinear(im, H) both use inverse warping:

  1. Push the four corners through \(H\) to get the output bounding box.
  2. For every output pixel \(p\), compute its pre-image \(H^{-1} p\) in the source image.
  3. Sample the source there.

Every output pixel gets exactly one lookup, so there are no holes. I treat \((0, 0)\) as the center of the top-left pixel.

  • Nearest neighbor rounds \((u, v)\) to the closest pixel center, \(\lfloor u + 0.5 \rfloor\), and copies it.
  • Bilinear takes the four surrounding pixel centers \((u_0, v_0) \ldots (u_0{+}1, v_0{+}1)\) and weights each by the area of the opposite sub-rectangle: \(I = (1-\alpha)(1-\beta) I_{00} + \alpha (1-\beta) I_{10} + (1-\alpha)\beta I_{01} + \alpha\beta I_{11}\), with \(\alpha = u - u_0\) and \(\beta = v - v_0\). A sample counts as valid only if it lies inside the pixel-center grid, \(0 \le u \le w-1\).

Both functions return an alpha channel (1 where the pre-image is inside the source, 0 elsewhere), so later steps can tell "black pixel" from "no data". Rows are processed in chunks so that large canvases fit in memory.

One subtlety came up with ground-plane rectification. If the warp's vanishing line crosses the image, part of the image lands "behind the camera" and the warped image is unbounded. A homography is only defined up to sign, so I orient \(H\) so that the image center has a positive homogeneous \(w\). An output pixel is then valid only if its pre-image's \(w\) is positive, which drops pixels on the wrong side of the horizon instead of folding them back into the picture.

Rectification

To test the warping, I map the four corners of a known rectangle (in the order top-left, top-right, bottom-right, bottom-left) onto an axis-aligned rectangle with the object's real aspect ratio. The target rectangle is centered on the object with the same area, so the result stays at about the input resolution. I located the corners on zoomed-in crops. The lid has rounded corners, so I fit a line to each edge and intersected them.

Each rectification is checked by things that weren't used to compute it:

  • Door: the door frame and the wall edges beside it also come out vertical.
  • TV: the wall paneling and the shelf below come out horizontal.
  • Lid: the second lid next to it becomes a rectangle with right-angle corners, the crosswalk stripes come out parallel, and the red curb comes out straight.

The black region in the lid result is ground outside the original photo. The region above the street is cut off at the horizon.

Quality: where the warp magnifies, nearest neighbor duplicates source pixels. You can see blocky stair-steps on the TV's mountains and furniture and jagged diagonal edges on the lid. Bilinear blends the four neighbors, so edges and gradients stay smooth. It is slightly softer, because each output pixel is a weighted average, but at 1:1 scale it looks much more natural. In regions the warp shrinks, the two look closer, and both can alias because neither one pre-filters.

Speed: bilinear consistently takes about 2.2× as long. It reads four pixels instead of one and does three interpolations per channel. The cost of computing \(H^{-1}p\) for each pixel is the same for both. For the mosaics I use bilinear everywhere, since the extra fraction of a second is worth avoiding the jaggies.

A.4: Blending the Images into a Mosaic

Once every photo has a homography into a common frame, making a mosaic has two parts. The geometric part puts every photo in the right place, using the warping from A.3. The photometric part decides what color each canvas pixel gets where two or more photos overlap. Even with perfect alignment, the photos never agree exactly in the overlaps:

  • Exposure: the phone meters every shot on its own, so the same wall comes out at different brightness.
  • Vignetting: lenses darken toward the corners, so a pixel near one photo's edge is darker than the same point near another photo's center.
  • Small misalignments: a pixel or two of registration error, or parallax from objects close to the camera.

If one photo simply overwrites the next, all of these show up as a hard line along the border of the top photo. The goal of blending is to spread the change from one photo to the next over a wide enough region that the eye doesn't see a line, without blurring or doubling fine detail.

Procedure. I use the one-shot approach: first find the size of the final canvas, then warp every photo onto it, then blend the stack. To keep things fast, everything runs at half resolution (1080×1920 per photo).

  1. Reference frame. The middle photo stays unwarped (for set 3, the mid-left photo), and every other photo is warped into its frame. Picking a photo in the middle keeps the outer photos as close to the reference as possible. This matters because a planar projection stretches an image more the further it is rotated from the reference, and the stretch goes to infinity at 90°. Not every photo overlaps the reference directly, so I chain the pairwise homographies. For example, the rightmost photo in set 3 goes right → mid-right → mid-left: \[ H_{\text{right}\to\text{ref}} = H_{\text{mid-right}\to\text{mid-left}}\; H_{\text{right}\to\text{mid-right}} \] Because the homographies are multiplied together first, each photo is resampled only once. Warping the warped image again would blur it a second time.
  2. Canvas. I push each photo's four corners through its homography. The canvas is the union of the resulting bounding boxes: 2403×2434, 2361×2534 and 2742×3012 for the three sets. warpImageBilinear warps each photo into its own bounding box and returns that box's offset, which says where the result sits on the canvas.
  3. Weights. Each photo gets a weight map that is 1 at its center and falls linearly to 0 at every edge: \[ w(x, y) = \Big(1 - \big|\tfrac{2x}{W-1} - 1\big|\Big)\Big(1 - \big|\tfrac{2y}{H-1} - 1\big|\Big) \] This encodes the idea that a photo is most trustworthy near its center (least vignetting, least lens distortion) and should fade out before its border. At the border, its weight is exactly 0, so its edge can never show up as a line. I use the product of a horizontal and a vertical tent rather than the distance to the nearest edge (bwdist). On a rectangle, the nearest-edge distance has creases along the diagonals, and those creases cause the wedge-shaped artifacts the assignment warns about. The product is smooth everywhere. I append the weight map to the photo as a fourth channel before warping, so it goes through exactly the same bilinear lookup as the colors and stays aligned with them.
  4. Exposure. Weighted averaging spreads a brightness difference across the overlap, but if the difference is large, a slow gradient across the overlap is still visible. So I first match exposures with a gain per color channel. Starting at the reference (gain 1) and working outward, each new photo \(i\) gets \[ g_i = \frac{\sum_{p \in \text{overlap}} g_j\, I_j(p)}{\sum_{p \in \text{overlap}} I_i(p)} \] Here the sums run over all pixels where photo \(i\) and an already-placed photo \(j\) both have data. This makes photo \(i\)'s mean color in the overlap equal to that of the photos already placed. Sets 1 and 2 needed only small corrections (0.92–1.0). In set 3, night mode exposed the mid-left photo much darker than its neighbors, which gave gains of ×1.4–1.6 for the left photo and ×0.8 for the right two.
  5. Blend. I compare three ways of combining the stack:
    • Overwrite pastes the photos from left to right, each covering the previous one. This is the baseline that shows what goes wrong without blending.
    • Feather sets each canvas pixel to the weighted average of every photo that covers it: \[ I(p) = \frac{\sum_i w_i(p)\, I_i(p)}{\sum_i w_i(p)} \] Dividing by the total weight matters. Where only one photo covers a pixel, the result is just that photo, whatever its weight. In an overlap, the result slides smoothly from one photo to the other as their weights cross. The catch is that the transition is as wide as the overlap, hundreds of pixels. If the photos are off by even a few pixels, every edge in that band shows up twice, faded: ghosting.
    • Two-band fixes this with a 2-level Laplacian pyramid. The idea comes from multiresolution blending in project 2: coarse structure should transition over a wide region, but fine detail should transition over a narrow one. Each warped photo is split into two bands:
      • a low band \(L_i = G_\sigma * I_i\) (Gaussian, \(\sigma = 6\) px), holding brightness, color and large shapes;
      • a high band \(H_i = I_i - L_i\), holding edges and texture.
      The low bands are feathered exactly as above, so exposure and color still change gradually across the whole overlap. For the high bands, each pixel takes the band from the single photo with the largest weight there (\(\arg\max_i w_i\)), so fine detail is never averaged and can't ghost. The switch happens along the line where two photos' weights are equal, roughly halfway between their centers. The result is \(\text{feather}(L_i) + H_{\arg\max_i w_i}\).
      One detail: blurring a warped photo naively mixes in the black canvas around it, which would darken its low band near its border and leave a dark halo. Instead, I blur with a normalized convolution, \(L_i = G_\sigma * (\alpha_i I_i) \,/\, G_\sigma * \alpha_i\), using the alpha channel from the warp. This averages only over pixels where the photo has data.

I add the photos one at a time into running sums: \(\sum w_i I_i\), \(\sum w_i\), and for two-band the current best weight with its high band. Memory therefore depends on the canvas size and not on the number of photos.

No single method wins everywhere, and each one's failure mode follows from how wide its transition is. Overwrite has no transition at all. Feathering uses the whole overlap, which hides exposure differences well but ghosts any misalignment. Two-band picks the width per frequency: wide for brightness and color, a single line for detail. It is the best default when there is any misalignment, as with the parallax in set 1. When the photos are well aligned but were processed differently, as in the night set, plain feathering can look slightly better. The improvements I'd try next are more pyramid levels, so the switch for mid-frequency detail can be a few pixels wide instead of a single line, and choosing the high-band seam along a path where the photos agree (a graph-cut seam), instead of the fixed equal-weight line.

A.5 Bells & Whistles: Cylindrical and Spherical Mosaics

A planar mosaic stretches whatever is far from the reference. Projecting onto a cylinder instead keeps the stretching uniform across the panorama. For that I need the camera itself (focal length and rotations), not just homographies.

  1. Camera model. For a camera that only rotates, \(H = K R K^{-1}\) with \(K = \begin{bmatrix} f & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).
    • Initialization: grid-search \(f\), assume the principal point is the image center, and project each \(K^{-1} H K\) to its nearest rotation (SVD). This explains the correspondences only to within 41 px on average.
    • Refinement: a small bundle adjustment (scipy.optimize.least_squares) jointly fits \(f\), \((c_x, c_y)\) and every photo's rotation, minimizing the reprojection error of all 140 correspondences, mapped in both directions. \(f\) and \((c_x, c_y)\) are shared across all three sets, since they were taken with the same phone. The result is \(f = 2354\) px and a principal point of \((1072, 1904)\), 17 px from the image center, with a mean error of 2.3 px. That is close to the 1.2–1.7 px of the unconstrained 8-parameter homographies.
    Before the refinement, the cylindrical mosaics had 10–20 px seams.
  2. Straightening. I use the mean panning axis of the rotations as the cylinder's axis instead of the reference camera's "up". This corrects a 1.5–3° tilt of the reference shots, which would otherwise make the horizon bend into a wave.
  3. Inverse sampling from the originals. For each output pixel \((u, v)\):
    1. Form the 3D ray: \(\theta = u/f\), \(\mathbf{d} = (\sin\theta,\; v/f,\; \cos\theta)\) for the cylinder, or \((\cos\phi\sin\theta,\; \sin\phi,\; \cos\phi\cos\theta)\) with \(\phi = v/f\) for the sphere.
    2. Rotate it into camera \(i\): \(R_i^{\top} W^{\top} \mathbf{d}\).
    3. Project with \(K\) and sample the original, full-resolution photo bilinearly.
    Each photo is resampled exactly once, and the result is at full resolution (about 4000×3900). The cylinder radius is \(f\), so the center of the panorama has the same pixel density as the photos.
  4. Blending. I reuse the same gain compensation and two-band blend. The tent weights are sampled along with the colors.

Compared with the planar mosaic, the cylinder gives every degree of pan the same width. The right-hand building and crosswalk in set 3 are no longer stretched outward, and the overall shape is a near-rectangle instead of a bow tie. The trade-off is that straight horizontal lines away from the horizon now curve, like the sidewalk edge and the ceiling in the indoor sets, because a cylinder can't keep both those lines and the verticals straight. The sphere also compresses the top and bottom, which the tall portrait photos make noticeable (it is about 650 px shorter). For these 90–95° horizontal panoramas, the cylinder looks the most natural.

What I Learned

The most useful lesson was how much depends on getting the geometry exactly right before any blending. A homography that is off by a couple of pixels produces ghosting that no blend can hide, and parallax cannot be fixed by any homography. Blending only hides the small leftover differences: feathering handles exposure, and the two-band split keeps fine detail from ghosting. Each method has its own failure mode, and the close-ups made those easy to see.

I was also surprised that a homography is "just" \(K R K^{-1}\) when the camera only rotates. Once I fit the focal length and rotations directly, the same correspondences gave me a camera model good to about 2 px, and the cylindrical and spherical panoramas came almost for free. The inverse-warping loop stays the same and only the function that maps an output pixel to a ray changes.
